Lash Radar

Lash courses go stale fast. I built Lash Radar to see what a lash course looks like when it keeps up: the lessons, the tools artists reach for at the chair, and training that tracks what clients are searching for.

/ / How I built it

Five steps. Same as every project.

This is the way I work on everything, and here's what each step looked like for a lash course.

01 / Watch

Artists with full hands and a phone on the tray.

The people using this spend the day with both hands on a client and a phone on the tray. So everything had to fit a phone, install like an app, and keep each job one tap away.

02 / Find the friction

PDFs nobody finishes.

Lash courses tend to sit in PDF files. Nobody can tell who finished, where students got stuck, or whether a lesson worked.

03 / Map it

Short lessons, plus the tools they reach for mid-appointment.

Courses break into short lessons inside the app, where you can see where students drop off and collect their feedback, so a weak lesson gets fixed instead of sitting in a file. Next to them sit the tools working artists reach for mid-appointment: lash weight math, glue cure and freshness, cost per set, a reaction triage, an eye mapper, and a vault of Texas rules for sanitation and scope. The tools give artists a reason to open the app after the course is done. Course lessons aren't in this public demo.

04 / Build it

The app, the station tools, and the trend radar.

Then I built it: the app itself, the station tools, and the trend radar that ties training to live search data so it never goes stale. The radar is the part I'm proudest of.

05 / Test it

Honest gaps.

It's a prototype. It needs an internet connection, and checklists and logs save to each phone without syncing between artists. Testing with working lash artists comes before it ships.

The core idea / A course that stays current

Courses go out of date the day you finish them.

That was the problem I kept coming back to before I built anything. Lash trends move fast, and technology moves faster. A course written this month is behind by next month. So the question became: how do you build training that doesn't have to be rebuilt every time the industry shifts?

Lash Radar answers it with search data. At the start of each week the studio picks the keywords it cares about, or lets the app pick them, and narrows the results to its own city or region. The app pulls that week's most searched lash topics from Google search trends, ranks what's rising, and turns each one into a short tutorial.

The course stops being a finished product. It's living: what clients are searching for this week becomes what the artists learn this week. The artists can edit any tutorial before they use it.

In this public demo the trends are sample data, labeled that way in the app. A live version would run on a studio's own Google account. Nothing here is connected to real search data.

/ / / The guardrails

Built for people with a license on the line.

A tool for licensed professionals has to earn trust before it earns screen time. These are the calls that keep it safe to use.

Rules from the source

No rule written from memory.

Every Texas rule in the Vault links to its official source and shows when it was checked. Anything the sources don't settle is flagged for a direct answer from the state or an attorney.

Scope on every trend

Popular doesn't mean allowed.

Lash lifts and tints are outside the Texas eyelash extension license. Any trend that touches them carries a warning and links to the rule, so nobody books a service their license doesn't cover.

Triage, not diagnosis

It sorts and refers out.

Eye reactions can't be undone. The triage tool helps an artist judge how urgent a reaction looks and who to send the client to. It never diagnoses and never suggests medication.

Their craft, their call

The expertise belongs to the artists.

Technique notes and tutorials are drafts for the studio's own educators to rewrite in their own words. Nothing here replaces your school's own procedures.
