Lash Radar

Lash courses go stale fast. I built Lash Radar to see what a lash course looks like when it keeps up. It holds the courses, the tools artists reach for at the chair, and training that keeps up with what clients are searching for.

/ / How I built it

Four steps. Same as every project.

This is the way I work on everything, and here's what each step looked like for a lash studio.

01 / Move into their world

PDFs nobody finishes, and artists with full hands.

The courses sat in PDF files. Nobody could tell who finished, where students got stuck, or whether a lesson worked. And the people using it spend the day with both hands on a client and a phone on the tray. So everything had to fit a phone, install like an app, and keep each job one tap away.

02 / Build the foundation

The courses are the heart of it.

Their classic, volume and other foundation courses move out of the PDFs and into the app as short lessons. In an app, you can see where students drop off and collect their feedback, so a weak lesson gets fixed instead of sitting in a file. The full course content stays private, so it isn't in this public demo.

03 / Add what fits

A reason to open it after the course is done.

Their students are mostly working lash artists. Once they finish a course, a course app has nothing left to offer them. So I added what they reach for mid-appointment: lash weight math, glue cure and freshness, cost per set, a reaction triage, an eye mapper, and a vault of Texas rules for sanitation and scope. The tools keep them coming back, and every visit puts the studio's training in front of them.

04 / Connect it

Hook it to the world outside the studio.

The last step ties the app to live data so the training never goes stale. That's the trend radar, and it's the part I'm proudest of.

The core idea / A course that stays current

Courses go out of date the day you finish them.

That was the problem I kept coming back to before I built anything. Lash trends move fast, and technology moves faster. A course written this month is behind by next month. So the question became: how do you build training that doesn't have to be rebuilt every time the industry shifts?

Lash Radar answers it with search data. At the start of each week the studio picks the keywords it cares about, or lets the app pick them, and narrows the results to its own city or region. The app pulls that week's most searched lash topics from Google search trends, ranks what's rising, and turns each one into a short tutorial.

The course stops being a finished product. It's living: what clients are searching for this week becomes what the artists learn this week. The artists can edit any tutorial before they use it.

In this public demo the trends are sample data, labeled that way in the app. The live version runs on the studio's own Google account, so nothing here is connected to real search data.

/ / / The guardrails

Built for people with a license on the line.

A tool for licensed professionals has to earn trust before it earns screen time. These are the calls that keep it safe to use.

Rules from the source

No rule written from memory.

Every Texas rule in the Vault links to TDLR or the Texas Administrative Code and shows when it was checked. Anything the sources don't settle is flagged for a direct answer from TDLR or an attorney.

Scope on every trend

Popular doesn't mean allowed.

Lash lifts and tints are outside the Texas eyelash extension license. Any trend that touches them carries a warning and links to the rule, so nobody books a service their license doesn't cover.

Triage, not diagnosis

It sorts and refers out.

Eye reactions can't be undone. The triage tool helps an artist judge how urgent a reaction looks and who to send the client to. It never diagnoses and never suggests medication.

Their craft, their call

The expertise belongs to the artists.

Technique notes and tutorials are drafts for the studio's own educators to rewrite in their own words, and nothing in the app claims to be state approved training.

Honest gaps

What the prototype doesn't do yet.

It needs an internet connection, and checklists and logs save to each phone without syncing between artists. Both are next if the studio takes it into daily use.
